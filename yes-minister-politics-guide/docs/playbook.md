# The Playbook — How to Play Politics, Whitehall-Style

This is the practical half. It is written from the point of view of the **permanent
operator** (the Sir Humphrey position) — the person who stays while ministers come and go —
because that is where most of the show's best moves come from. Where a move is more useful
to the *minister* (the person trying to actually get something done), it is marked
**[For the principal]**.

---

## Part 1 — The four core principles

Everything else is a variation on these.

### 1. Control the information

Whoever decides *what the decision-maker sees* effectively makes the decision. You rarely
need to win an argument if you control which arguments ever reach the table.

- Summarise. A summary is where the real power lies — what you leave out never gets debated.
- Volume is a weapon. A genuinely important document hidden inside 200 pages of routine
  paper has been "disclosed" without ever being read.
- Time the flow. Information delivered too late to act on has been provided, technically.

### 2. Control the agenda

If you control *what gets discussed, in what order, and when*, you don't need to control
the vote.

- Put the item you want passed straight after a long, exhausting item.
- Put the item you want killed last, when everyone wants to go home.
- "We're running short of time" is a complete strategy on its own.

### 3. Never say "no" — make "yes" impossible

Direct refusal creates a fight you might lose and a grievance that lasts. Far safer to
**agree enthusiastically in principle** and then ensure the practicalities never line up.

- "Minister, that is a *very* courageous decision." (Translation: it will end your career —
  see the glossary.)
- Agree to the goal, dispute only the timing, the funding, the legal advice, and the
  implementation. Forever.

### 4. Be the one who stays

Your single greatest structural advantage is permanence. Ministers are temporary; the
machine is not. You can outlast almost any initiative simply by surviving it.

- "This too shall pass" applied to people. Wait out the enthusiast.
- Never burn a relationship you'll still have after the current crisis.

## Part 2 — The classic manoeuvres

### Strategic delay (the master tactic)

Delay is the safest weapon in politics because **nobody can be blamed for a decision that
was never quite made.** You are not opposing anything — you are merely being thorough.

Ways to delay while appearing diligent:

- **Commission a study.** Reviews take time, and by the time they report, the political
  moment has passed.
- **Refer it to a committee.** Especially an interdepartmental one — coordination is slow
  by design.
- **Request further consultation** with stakeholders, the unions, the Treasury, legal,
  the devolved bodies, allies abroad — each adds weeks.
- **Discover a procedural problem** that must be resolved "properly" before proceeding.
- **Wait for the right moment**, which, with care, never arrives.

### The Four-Stage Strategy (for handling a crisis or unwanted disclosure)

The show's most famous escalation ladder for killing momentum on something you'd rather
nobody acted on:

1. **Stage one:** "Nothing is going to happen." (Deny there is an issue at all.)
2. **Stage two:** "Something *may* be about to happen, but we should do nothing about it."
3. **Stage three:** "Maybe we should do something, but there is nothing we *can* do."
4. **Stage four:** "Maybe there was something we could have done, but it's too late now."

By the time you reach stage four, the decision has been made by the clock, and no one
chose it.

### Framing so only one answer survives

Don't argue against the option you dislike — **arrange the choices** so the answer you want
is the only reasonable one.

- Offer three options: one obviously disastrous, one obviously absurd, and the one you
  wanted all along, which now looks like wise moderation.
- Define the question narrowly enough that your preferred answer is the only thing that fits.
- Attach the alternative to a consequence the decision-maker fears (cost, scandal, a hostile
  headline, a powerful enemy).

### Burying it (committees, reviews, working groups)

A subject sent to the right committee may never be seen again. Useful properties of
committees: they diffuse responsibility, they move at the speed of their slowest member,
and their conclusions can be "noted" rather than "actioned."

- Pack the working group so it cannot reach a clean conclusion.
- Give it terms of reference so broad it can never finish, or so narrow it can never matter.
- Ensure its report lands during recess, a reshuffle, or a bigger news day.

### The non-answer

The art of speaking at length while committing to nothing — so you are "on the record"
as having responded.

- Answer a slightly different question than the one asked.
- "I'm glad you asked that" → followed by background, context, and the importance of getting
  it right, but never the answer.
- "Let me be absolutely clear" usually precedes the opposite.
- Use the passive voice to remove the actor: "mistakes were made," not "I made a mistake."

### Plausible deniability

Arrange matters so that, if it goes wrong, you genuinely did not *technically* know.

- Don't put the risky part in writing; raise it only in conversation.
- Let the decision be "taken at official level" so the principal can disown it.
- Keep the awkward detail in an annex the principal "was free to read."

### Managing the principal (your minister / boss) **[works both directions]**

The civil servant manages the minister; the wise minister learns to manage back.

- **Keep them busy.** A minister buried in red boxes and ceremonial duties has no time to
  cause trouble.
- **Give them wins that don't matter** so they feel effective without changing anything.
- **Flatter the instinct, redirect the action.** "Exactly so, Minister — and the *best* way
  to achieve that is…"
- **[For the principal]** Demand the options *and* the recommendation in writing. Ask "what
  are you not telling me?" Cultivate an independent source of advice (a special adviser) so
  you are not wholly dependent on the machine.

### Leaks and the press **[works both directions]**

- A leak is "an unauthorised disclosure"; a briefing is "keeping the press informed." Same
  act, different label depending on who did it.
- Float an unpopular idea anonymously to test reaction; if it dies, you never proposed it.
- Time a friendly story to drown an unfriendly one.

### The reshuffle and survival **[For the principal]**

- Your job in week one is to still have the job in week two. Pick few fights; win those.
- Take credit publicly, share blame privately, and never humiliate someone who'll outlast you.

## Part 3 — Quick cheat-sheet

| If you want to… | …then do this |
| --- | --- |
| Kill a proposal without being blamed | Refer it to an interdepartmental committee with broad terms of reference |
| Stop action while looking diligent | Commission a review / call for further consultation |
| Avoid committing to anything | Give the non-answer; agree in principle, dispute the practicalities |
| Get your option chosen | Frame it as the moderate one between a disaster and an absurdity |
| Defuse a brewing crisis | Run the Four-Stage Strategy until the clock decides |
| Stay safe if it goes wrong | Keep the risky part verbal; let it be "decided at official level" |
| Neutralise an over-eager principal | Bury them in boxes; give them harmless wins |
| Warn that something is career-ending | Call it "courageous" / "bold" |
| Win without winning the vote | Control the agenda order and the timing |
| **[Principal] avoid being managed** | Demand options *and* a written recommendation; keep an independent adviser |
| **[Principal] get something actually done** | Set a hard deadline, attach a name to delivery, and check the annexes |
